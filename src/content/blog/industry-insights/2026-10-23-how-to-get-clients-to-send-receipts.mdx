The bookkeeping is ready to go, and then it stalls, because a handful of receipts are still in a client's glovebox, a coat pocket, or the bin. A missing receipt usually starts as a purchase the client meant to send in and didn't. You find out at month end, when the payment is on the bank statement with nothing behind it.

So there are two places to work on it: the routine that gets receipts in, so fewer go missing, and how you ask about the ones that still are. Asking louder rarely helps with either. It's more useful to start with why receipts don't arrive in the first place.

## Why clients don't send their receipts

It's tempting to put it down to disorganisation, but that's rarely the whole story. Some clients who run tidy, successful businesses are still hopeless at sending in a receipt. The reasons are usually practical, and once you see them the fixes are easier to spot.

### It's low-value admin to them

To you, a receipt is a record that has to exist. To your client, it's a small piece of paper that has already done its job at the till. Sending it on does nothing visible for their business, so it drops to the bottom of a long list of things that feel more urgent. That's less laziness than a fair reading of their own priorities, and an approach that ignores it tends to fail.

### The method is more effort than it looks

A lot of receipt collection quietly asks the client to do more than it seems. Download this app, create an account, remember a password, find the right email address, log into a portal. Each step is small on its own, and each one loses a few more people. Someone standing in a car park with a fading till receipt will put it off if sending it means any of that, and later often means never.

### They're asked at the wrong time

Receipts are usually requested weeks after the purchase, at month end or, worse, at year end. By then the memory has faded, the paper may be gone, and the client is being asked to reconstruct something they barely remember. The best moment to capture a receipt is when it's handed over, and a request at month end has already missed it.

### The person with the receipt is often not the person you deal with

The director who takes your calls is often not the one buying fuel, materials, or a round of coffees for the team. That happens on site, on the road, or across a counter. It's done by an engineer, a manager, or an assistant who may never speak to the practice at all. Route everything through your main contact and that one person becomes the collection point for everyone else, which is a bottleneck you can see coming.

## Should you chase at all?

Some bookkeepers have stopped chasing altogether. Their engagement letter makes receipts the client's responsibility. They ask once, and anything still unexplained goes to a suspense account for the client to deal with.

There's a lot to be said for that. The engagement letter is the right place for the responsibility: it sets the expectation before any habits form, and it gives you something to point to when a client is surprised later. A suspense account is a fair home for a £4 parking charge, or for the client who never replies to anything.

What it doesn't do is make the question go away. The balance still has to be cleared before the accounts are finished, so the question comes back at year end, when the client remembers least. For a VAT-registered client there's more at stake. HMRC expects a valid VAT invoice or receipt before the VAT on a purchase is reclaimed. Some practices use that as the stick: no receipt, and the purchase is recorded with no VAT, so the client can't reclaim it. That's a fair consequence, but it's money the client loses for want of a piece of paper.

So keep the clause in the engagement letter, and ask once. Then make answering cheap enough that the client actually does it.

## Getting routine receipts in

None of this needs a clever trick. It mostly comes down to taking effort away and asking at a better moment, whatever tool you use.

- **Set the expectation at the start:** the easiest time to agree how receipts come in is during onboarding, before any habits have formed. Starting a routine with a new client is much easier than introducing one to a client of three years.
- **Take away every step you can:** every account, download, and login is a place to lose someone. Judge a method by the number of steps it asks of the person least invested in the outcome.
- **Capture at the moment of purchase:** a photo taken while the receipt is still in someone's hand beats a search through a shoebox in March. Anything that lets a person deal with a receipt on the spot, rather than saving it up, will lose fewer.
- **Meet people on a channel they already use:** their phone is in their pocket, with email and a messaging app already on it. Asking them to adopt something new adds a hurdle at exactly the point where hurdles cost you.
- **Let more than one person send:** the engineer who bought the parts should be able to send the receipt directly, without it passing through the director's inbox first.
- **Keep a light, regular rhythm:** a small monthly nudge does more than an annual scramble. It keeps volumes manageable, keeps receipts fresh, and avoids the year-end pile-up that's stressful for everyone and error-prone on top.

## Asking about what's still missing

However good the routine, some payments will reach the bank statement with nothing behind them. The usual way to ask about them is an email with a list, and then a thread of replies.

In our own practice, it was a spreadsheet for each client. After the bookkeeping, we downloaded the unreconciled payments into it and deleted every line the client didn't need to see, often the same lines we'd deleted the month before. The client emailed receipts and left comments in the sheet. It worked, slowly, and it was easy to lose track of what had been answered.

What we'd look for now, whatever you use:

- **One list per client, not a thread:** the client answers against each payment, and you can both see what's still open.
- **Only the payments they need to explain:** leave out salaries and transfers between their own accounts. A shorter list is more likely to get answered.
- **A list that stays current:** if it always shows what's still outstanding, nobody has to work out which email is the latest.
- **A note counts as an answer:** sometimes there is no receipt, and "client lunch, receipt lost" is something you can work with.
- **Something your contact can pass on:** the person who made the purchase should be able to answer without needing a login.
